Near the onset of turbulence in pipe flow, turbulence appears as localized patches. At lower Reynolds numbers these are *puffs*, which can decay or split; at higher Reynolds numbers they become *slugs*, turbulent regions whose fronts move apart so that the turbulent region keeps growing. The puff-splitting regime is now understood through directed percolation, but the crossover from puffs to slugs is less well understood.

This project studies the sequence

decay → puff splitting (directed percolation) → jammed puffs → weak slug → strong slug

in quasi-one-dimensional stochastic models of turbulence. These models follow the predator-prey (trophic) picture, in which turbulent activity feeds on the energy carried by the laminar shear flow. The main model is the three-trophic-state model proposed by Xueying Wang. We compare it with hard-core lattice models of puff dynamics in the spirit of Lemoult and collaborators.

The main finding so far is that two mechanisms had been conflated:

- **Hard-core exclusion causes jamming.** When a puff can only be born or hop into an empty site, the model gets a density cap and traffic-like (Burgers) flux, and jammed or crystalline puff trains can form.
- **Soft, resource-limited growth sets the shape of slugs.** In the full three-trophic model, turbulent activity is a continuous energy density born from a nutrient field, not a hard particle. The model produces puffs, weak slugs with sharp upstream fronts and nutrient-limited tails, and strong slugs, but in the tests so far it shows no true jammed crystal.

To tell these states apart we use structural and dynamical diagnostics together, never a spacetime plot alone. These include Lindemann spacing disorder, pair correlations and structure factors, hole statistics and lifetimes, front-velocity drift, interior extinction, and front-front correlations. The open problem is to quantify the transition from ordinary puff splitting to weak slugs through the rate at which daughter puffs cleanly separate, how fast the local nutrient recovers, and downstream branching rates.
